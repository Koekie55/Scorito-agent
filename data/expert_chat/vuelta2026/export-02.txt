Scorito-agent repository⁠￼

What it can currently do

The repo is essentially a Scorito cycling prediction/optimisation system with several independent data engines:

Component	What it does
Scorito API	Rider prices, qualities, points, teams, your team/selection and game rules
PCS	Historical races/stages, startlists, results and stage characteristics
CyclingOracle	Stage predictions/statistical information
Expert chat	Converts expert opinions into rider/stage signals
WielerFlits forum	Extracts and weights forum claims
Rider news	Incorporates current rider/team news
Stage predictor	Predicts likely top finishers for every stage
Stage scorer	Converts predicted performance into expected Scorito points
Optimiser	Finds the best 20-rider squad under the budget
Stage planner	Determines the optimal 9 riders + captain per stage
Validation	Backtests the PCS predictor against historical actual results

The architecture explicitly combines Scorito + CyclingOracle + PCS, with the goal of predicting stages and building the optimal Vuelta squad. 

The really interesting part

The recommend_vuelta_live.py script is effectively a live Vuelta decision engine.

It doesn’t just rank riders. It builds projected points from multiple signals, including:

* model-predicted points
* PCS predictions
* expert-chat intelligence
* WielerFlits/forum opinion
* rider news
* team bonuses
* red-jersey/team effects
* captain effects

The code explicitly loads the expert-chat intelligence, forum opinion and rider-news datasets and combines them into the stage projections. 

That means we can ask it questions like:

“For Stage 8, which of Groves, Aular and Oliveira has the highest probability of finishing in the Scorito top 20, and what is their expected Scorito value?”

rather than relying on generic cycling knowledge.

It can also optimise the entire tournament

This is probably the most valuable functionality for what you’ve been trying to do.

The exact optimiser evaluates a fixed 20-rider squad across all stages, then determines the best 9 riders for each stage and captain. The code calculates stage points, team bonuses, red-jersey bonuses and captain bonuses separately. 

So conceptually:

20 riders → 21 stages → 9 starters/stage → captain/stage → maximise total Scorito points

That’s substantially better than simply selecting the 20 riders with the highest individual projected scores.

It understands value as well

The stage_study functionality calculates both total projected/realised points and points per €1m, allowing us to distinguish:

* best riders
* best bargains
* riders who are expensive but still worth it
* riders who look attractive but don’t actually generate enough points

It also analyses which rider archetypes perform best on different terrain types. 

And importantly: it has a validation framework

This is something I particularly like.

The repository doesn’t simply claim that its predictor works. validate_pcs_predictor.py performs leave-one-stage-out validation, comparing predicted rider rankings with actual Scorito points and calculating rank correlation/top-10 hit rates. 

That gives us the possibility of asking:

“Which model is actually better?”

rather than blindly trusting the fanciest-looking prediction.

What I can do with it for you

There are essentially four levels of analysis I can now exploit.

1. Individual rider probability

For example:

Groves vs Aular vs Oliveira

I can determine:

* expected finishing position
* probability top 5
* probability top 10
* probability top 20
* expected Scorito points
* expected captain points
* stage-specific suitability
* confidence
* value relative to price

This is exactly the sort of analysis you were asking for earlier.

2. Stage-by-stage top 20

For all 21 Vuelta stages, we can generate:

Stage	#1	#2	#3	…	#20
1					
2					
…					
21					

But importantly, we can attach probabilities rather than pretending the ranking is certain.

For example:

Groves — 68% top 20
Aular — 61%
Oliveira — 44%

That is much more useful for Scorito.

3. Optimal 20-man squad

This is where the repo becomes genuinely powerful.

We can optimise:

Expected total Scorito points over the entire Vuelta

subject to:

* budget
* exactly 20 riders
* availability
* stage participation
* 9 riders per stage
* captain selection
* classification/jersey points
* team bonuses

The optimiser already has much of this machinery. 

4. Live daily optimisation

And this is probably the best way to use it during the Vuelta.

Before each stage:

Refresh

→ new startlist
→ rider news
→ team tactics
→ expert opinions
→ PCS information
→ current classification
→ injuries/form
→ stage prediction
→ Scorito scoring model

Then calculate:

“Who are my optimal 9 + captain TODAY?”

The repo already contains scripts such as recommend_vuelta_live.py, recommend_enrolled.py, recommend_stage_plan.py, rider_news.py and refresh_vuelta_stage_predictions.py for essentially this workflow.

One important limitation

There is one major practical constraint: PCS is Cloudflare protected.

The repository itself documents that PCS returns Cloudflare errors from datacentre IPs and therefore deliberately uses aggressive caching; the author expects deep scraping to run from a residential/home connection. 

So I can inspect and reason over the repo/data available through GitHub, but I cannot simply execute its entire local PCS scraping environment inside this chat and pretend that I did.

That’s important because I don’t want to repeat the problem we had earlier where the analysis appeared to work but was actually operating on incomplete/old data.

* current form
* rider news
* team tactics
* expert/forum information
* Monte Carlo simulation
* exact 20-man optimisation**

Then we can answer the question you actually care about:

“Which 20 riders maximise my expected Scorito score, and for every stage which 9 should I put in, who should be captain, and what is the probability distribution of my total score?”

That is far more powerful than simply asking “who will win Stage 8?”

Dat komt doordat UAE in de voorspelling de winnaar is van stages:

1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 12, 13, 14, 18, 19 en 20

en daarnaast gaat de agent ervan uit dat Pogačar na iedere stage rood houdt. Daardoor krijgt een geselecteerde UAE-renner telkens +8, plus nog +10 op UAE-winstdagen.

Maar hier zit de grote caveat

De 376 vs 256 vs 202 is géén volledig onafhankelijke Monte-Carlo expected score. Het is de score onder de vrij sterke agent-aanname:

Pogačar houdt rood na iedere stage.

En de teambonus is bovendien alleen relevant als de renner daadwerkelijk in je 9 voor die stage zit. De agent-code behandelt de teambonus daarom als een conditional upside en niet als zekere basis voor de lineup-optimalisatie.

Mijn voorlopige conclusie:
Als je puur kijkt naar de raw agent-data, is Oliveira veel interessanter dan ik eerder inschatte. Zijn UAE-bonus kan Groves’ enorme individuele voordeel volledig omdraaien.

De volgende stap die ik zou doen is daarom nog interessanter: niet 376 als vaste score nemen, maar per stage de kans berekenen dat Oliveira daadwerkelijk in jouw 9 zit én UAE wint/red houdt, en hetzelfde voor Groves/Aular. Dan krijgen we bijvoorbeeld:

So `stage_rider_analytics.csv` has them 1st and 3rd. They disappear only in `build_stage_top20`, which re-scores on top of that ranking.

## The specific line that does it

Stage 2 is hilly, `gradient_final_km` 3.6, expert notes type "Sprint / Hilly" — so `_is_reduced_sprint_stage` returns True and the `conversion_factor` branch fires. The first rule inside it:

```python
if pcs_gc >= 0.20 and pcs_sprint < 0.02:
    conversion_factor = 0.08
```

Pogačar: gc 0.946, sprint 0.0. Skjelmose: gc 0.389, sprint 0.0. Both get their score multiplied by 0.08 — a 92% cut. Pogačar's pre-penalty combined score is roughly 0.85; times 0.08 that's about 0.07, and the 20th-place cutoff was Camprubí at 0.094. He misses by that constant alone, nothing else. Same for Skjelmose. Eleven riders clear gc ≥ 0.20, and all of them have sprint 0.0, so the whole GC group is wiped from every stage classified this way.

## Why the penalty misfires

The gate assumes "no sprint signal" means "GC rider who won't contest a fast finish". It doesn't — the signal is just sparse. Only 25 of 184 riders have any sprint value at all, and only 14 clear the 0.02 threshold. Kaden Groves, a genuine bunch sprinter, has sprint 0.0 — he only survives because his classic/fast-finish path rescues him. The gate is separating riders who have PCS sprint-specialty data from riders who don't, not sprinters from climbers.

## Why Debruyne and Camprubí get through

They ride the survival machinery instead. Debruyne's `selective_result_score` is 1.0 and Camprubí's 0.92 — both maxed. Two reasons:

- Every rider has exactly 6 contextual results. Pogačar's six are mostly 2024 mountain wins (recency weight 0.13); Debruyne's and Camprubí's are 2026 (weight 0.60). Recent top-10s in weak fields outscore capped older wins in the strongest fields.
- TTT stage results leak in. Debruyne's "S1 (TTT) Barcelona, rank 7" is tagged `profile_type: hilly`, so a team time trial can feed a hilly selective-result score.

Camprubí's inputs are a 2nd in Locarno (field 0.70) and the Spanish national road race (field 0.42) — both real, neither evidence he beats Pogačar over 3,028 vm.

## What I'd change

- Log `conversion_factor` in the output dict. It's the single largest multiplier in the pipeline and it isn't written to the JSON or CSV, so nothing in the published data explains the exclusion. That's why the top-20 looked inexplicable.
- Replace the 0.08 cliff with something continuous, and gate it on evidence a rider actually loses time on 3-4% uphill finishes rather than on a missing sprint signal.
- Reconsider calling stage 2 a reduced sprint at all: uphill finish, 3.6% final km, 3,028 vm. A 214 km day at that vertical is where GC riders gain, not lose.
- Cap `selective_result_score` when the sample is thin, and filter `finish_type == "ttt"` out of both result scorers.
